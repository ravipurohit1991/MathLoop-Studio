# MathLoop Studio walkthrough

Narration: Microsoft Edge neural synthetic voice.

## 0:00 — What we will make

In this walkthrough, we will make a short mathematical film together. We will start with a ready-made monkey animation, change its text and timing, choose music, save the editable project, and export a video.

Then we will bring in our own SVG, explore three-dimensional films, arrange a three-sixty scene, and visit Nature Worlds. You can follow along using the example files linked in the description.

## 0:28 — Run the studio locally

To get started, install Node version twenty-two or newer and Git. Clone the repository, enter its folder, run npm ci to install the dependencies, then npm run dev. Open the local address shown here. The studio runs in your browser without an account or API key.

## 0:49 — Choose the right workspace

The three tabs represent three kinds of output. Story Studio creates portrait films. Three-sixty Studio places animated objects around a viewer. Nature Worlds uses photographic environments with animated weather. We will begin in Story Studio, where the same editing ideas are easiest to see.

## 1:11 — 1. Choose a starting film

The collection gives you fifteen starting films. Use the filters to browse two-dimensional drawings or three-dimensional sculptures. Each option already has an animation, chapters, colours, and a soundtrack. Choosing a film loads an editable starting point.

For our example, choose the Fourier monkey. We will keep its underlying animation and make the story our own. You do not need to write code for these edits.

## 1:40 — 2. Read the editor

Read the editor from left to right. Chapters form the story on the left. The centre is the actual film preview. The right panel edits the selected chapter. Click a chapter to jump to that moment, then look at its title and caption in the inspector.

## 1:57 — What do the rotating circles do?

Here is the mathematical idea behind the drawing. Each rotating circle contributes a vector. Add those vectors together, and the endpoint traces a path. More components can reproduce a more detailed outline. The chapters reveal this construction step by step, before showing the finished artwork.

## 2:18 — 3. Give a chapter a clear message

Let us rewrite the opening. The title supports separate lines, so I will use: One circle, many possibilities. The caption explains what the viewer should notice. Watch the centre preview update as the text changes.

## 2:34 — Give the message time to be read

Chapter duration controls how long this particular idea stays on screen. I will give the opening four seconds. Total length is different: it scales the timing of the entire film. The animation cues follow their chapters when you change the timing.

For a compact review export, set the total length to twenty seconds. Notice how the chapter timings update together. This is useful for trying an edit quickly; a slower teaching film can use a much longer duration.

## 3:06 — 4. Check the sequence

Use the playhead to inspect any point in the film. The numbered ribbon below it jumps between chapters. Previous frame and next frame are useful for checking a transition precisely. Press play to judge whether the text and animation have enough breathing room.

## 3:24 — 5. Set the look and delivery

Below the chapter settings are the film title, frame rate, delivery size, and colour palette. I am using twenty-four frames per second and a small portrait export for this demonstration. For a larger final upload, choose ten-eighty by nineteen-twenty.

The background, ink, accent, and guide swatches control the visual palette. Return to the beginning controls looping in the preview. Always inspect the completed picture after a change, especially if you choose colours with less contrast.

## 3:58 — 6. Choose music

Scroll to The Sound. These eight scores are generated by the app. Click a card to select and audition it. I will choose Still Water, then reduce the score volume. No score gives you a silent export. The chosen soundtrack is saved with the project.

## 4:15 — 7. Save an editable copy

Save project downloads a JSON file containing the editable recipe: the story, chapter text, timing, colours, and audio settings. Browser autosave is convenient, but it is not a portable backup. Keep this downloaded project whenever you want to return to an edit.

To check that it is reusable, I will open that same file again. The edited title and twenty-second timeline return. Saving the project and exporting a film are separate steps; one preserves the edit, the other creates something you can watch.

## 4:51 — 8. Export the MP4

Now click Export MP4. The app renders the frames and includes the selected score. The waiting interval is shortened here. When the download completes, open the MP4 and check the picture, timing, and sound before sharing it.

## 5:08 — Our exported film

Here is the exported file we just made. The text changes, chapter pacing, colour choice, and soundtrack all belong to this saved edit. I will let a few seconds play so you can hear the result.

## 5:32 — 9. Bring your own outline

You can also start from your own artwork. Turn an SVG into a story imports an outline and builds a Fourier animation from its contours. I am using a simple leaf from the example files. The import creates a new project, so save your current edit first.

Scrub forward to see the leaf reconstructed. Then edit the chapter wording just as we did for the monkey. This feature imports geometry, rather than reproducing every SVG styling effect. A clean outline is a useful first experiment.

## 6:07 — 10. Move from a drawing to a sculpture

Next, choose the three-dimensional whale. Its geometry has depth, so changing the camera changes how the shape reads. The chapter editor, timeline, music, and export workflow are the same. A three-dimensional subject still produces an ordinary portrait video here.

## 6:26 — Inspect depth, guides and the surface

Further down the inspector, construction guides can appear as rotating circles, sphere cages, or be hidden. You can also show the surface mesh. These views help explain how a form is built, or simplify it for the finished film.

The numeric camera controls let you orbit and tilt the view. I will change the yaw, then inspect the whale again. Use these controls to reveal the depth clearly instead of assuming the audience can infer it from one angle.

## 6:58 — 11. Go deeper with animation tracks

For more control, open Animation tracks. The JSON describes parameter values at moments within a chapter. Progress zero is the chapter start, and one is its end. Apply tracks validates your changes. This is optional: the ready-made films already include their animation tracks.

## 7:18 — How the source fits together

If you want to extend the code, start with the studio folder for the React interface, source stories for the film definitions, and source story for the timeline and rendering engine. The same time-based animation model drives preview and export. The command-line tools provide repeatable renders and batch workflows.

## 7:38 — 12. Arrange a room around the viewer

Now open Three-sixty Studio. Start with the aquarium, lanterns, or observatory. Here we arrange objects around a viewpoint. The audience can choose where to look in the exported spherical video. That is different from watching a camera angle that we fixed in a portrait film.

## 7:57 — Understand the two preview modes

In Look around mode, drag across the preview to turn your head inside the scene. Panorama shows the complete sphere laid flat in a two-to-one image. The distortion in that flat image is expected; a spherical player maps it back around the viewer.

## 8:14 — 13. Add and place a reel

The left panel lists the reels already in the sphere. Add another from the rack, then select it. Its caption and placement controls appear on the right. Heading moves it around the viewer, while elevation moves it above or below the horizon.

Size and distance affect how large it feels. Tilt and facing control its orientation. Select the reel again to turn the preview toward it, then inspect the panorama to check the overall arrangement. Leave space between objects so the scene stays readable.

## 8:49 — 14. Save a stage and choose an output

Save stage downloads the editable arrangement. The spherical export uses a two-to-one frame with projection metadata. The delivery selector includes thirty-eight-forty by nineteen-twenty. As with a story, set the duration, frame rate, palette, and music before exporting.

There is also a separate portrait companion button. It renders a guided tour of the same room for a vertical feed. The spherical film lets viewers look around; the companion chooses the camera path for them. Save both when you want those two viewing experiences.

## 9:26 — Two deliverables from a 360° stage

These are the included aquarium sample outputs. The wide frame contains every direction, while the portrait companion visits the artwork in sequence. They are separate files. This walkthrough itself is a standard landscape video; use the spherical sample to try interactive viewing.

## 9:46 — 15. Switch to photographic worlds

Nature Worlds is a different visual system. It combines photographic panoramas with procedural weather and synthesized ambience. Choose the seasonal world, rainforest, or ocean. These are environments to explore, rather than Fourier drawings to edit chapter by chapter.

## 10:06 — Look, turn and compare directions

Drag the picture or use the arrow keys to look around. The direction cards below jump to specific parts of the environment. Full panorama reveals the complete image. These controls help you inspect the world before deciding how much weather to add.

## 10:23 — 16. Set weather, duration and delivery

Weather intensity changes the animated effect. Set it low for a quieter scene or increase it to make the weather more visible. Loop length controls the exported duration, and panorama size controls resolution. The export contains the full sphere, regardless of which direction the preview faces.

## 10:45 — Understand ambience and spatial export

Listen to this direction auditions the ambience for the direction you face. Spatial export behaves differently: other directions remain audible as the listener turns. Browser support for four-channel audio varies. If the browser cannot encode it, use the native command-line renderer with FFmpeg.

## 11:06 — 17. Repeat a render from the terminal

The command line is useful for repeatable exports. Render a saved story project to reproduce your edit, or run collection to render the showcase films. The nature render command creates a spherical nature film with its spatial soundtrack through the native workflow. The readme documents the available commands and options.

## 11:29 — Make your first film

You now have the complete workflow. Choose a starting point, give each chapter one clear idea, check the pacing, add sound, save the editable project, and watch the exported film. Then try an SVG outline or build a scene around the viewer.
